\title{Infinite NPA depth at minimal semidefinite complexity}
\author{Anubhav Chaturvedi}
\affiliation{Division of Quantum Optics and Information, Institute of Theoretical Physics and Astrophysics, Faculty of Mathematics, Physics and Informatics, University of Gda\'nsk, 80-308 Gda\'nsk, Poland}
\date{28 October 2026}
\begin{abstract}
The quantum region of the CHSH value and the sum of two marginal averages requires infinite Navascués–Pironio–Acín depth, yet admits an exact finite semidefinite lift. We construct this lift by showing that every supporting bound is attained with equal measurement overlaps: one angle determines the entire boundary. Finite NPA certificates must remain valid for two independently varying angles, where the obstruction to finite depth survives. The same region has the smallest semidefinite extension degree possible for a nonpolyhedral set, separating infinite hierarchy depth from minimal semidefinite complexity.
\end{abstract}
\maketitle

Alice and Bob share a quantum state and each choose between two
measurements with outcomes $\pm1$. Their observables are $A_x,B_y$,
where $x,y\in\{0,1\}$. Write $a_x=\langle A_x\rangle$, $b_y=\langle B_y\rangle$ and
$E_{xy}=\langle A_xB_y\rangle$, and retain
\begin{equation}
 M=a_0+b_0,\qquad S=E_{00}+E_{01}+E_{10}-E_{11}.
 \label{eq:observed}
\end{equation}
$S$ is the CHSH value~\cite{CHSH}. Let $\W$ contain all pairs $(M,S)$
attainable with quantum states and local measurements. The two marginal
averages may differ; only their sum is retained. Shared randomness allows
convex mixtures of strategies, so $\W$ is convex.

The preceding Letter~\cite{prior} proved that every finite NPA
level~\cite{NPA2007,NPA2008} overestimates some supporting lines
$S+\alpha M$ near $\alpha=1$. Here we construct an exact finite lift
of this same region: membership in $\W$ is equivalent to finitely many
positive semidefinite (PSD) matrix constraints, with entries affine
(constant plus linear) in $M,S$ and auxiliary real variables.
Write $\W_L$ for its level-$L$ NPA projection. The semidefinite extension
degree is the smallest possible largest matrix size in such a lift. We show
\begin{equation}
 \W\subsetneq\W_L\quad\text{for every finite }L,
 \qquad \sxdeg(\W)=2.
 \label{eq:result}
\end{equation}
Our explicit lift uses matrices of sizes five and four. The optimal
size two follows from a theorem on planar convex sets.

\emph{Equal overlaps determine every supporting line.}---
A supporting line bounds $hS+\alpha M$ for fixed real coefficients
$h,\alpha$. Define the Bell operator
\[
 C_{\mathrm{CHSH}}=A_0B_0+A_0B_1+A_1B_0-A_1B_1,
\]
where $A_xB_y$ means $A_x\otimes B_y$, and set
$H=hC_{\mathrm{CHSH}}+\alpha(A_0+B_0)$, with identities on the other party
understood. The bound $hS+\alpha M\le\lambda$ holds in every state
exactly when $\lambda I-H\succeq0$; here $\succeq0$ means PSD.
Every binary measurement can be realized as a projective measurement
on a larger space. Jordan's lemma then decomposes each local pair into
blocks of dimension at most two. In each block choose axes so that
\begin{equation}
\begin{gathered}
A_0=B_0=Z,\\
 A_1=cZ+\sqrt{1-c^2}X,\quad
 B_1=dZ+\sqrt{1-d^2}X.
\end{gathered}
 \label{eq:angles}
\end{equation}
Here $X,Z$ are Pauli matrices and $c,d\in[-1,1]$ are the cosines
of the angles between each party's two measurement directions. These
are the measurement overlaps. Deterministic strategies attain the
local bound $\beta=2|h|+2|\alpha|$, so $\lambda\ge\beta$ is necessary.

For $\lambda\ge\beta$, a nonnegative determinant is enough to show
that $\lambda I-H$ is PSD. Put $\Omega=1+c+d-cd\in[-2,2]$ and expand
$\det(rI+\lambda I-H)=r^4+e_1r^3+e_2r^2+e_3r+D$:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
 e_1&=4\lambda,\qquad e_2=6\lambda^2-4\alpha^2-8h^2,\\
 e_3&=4\lambda^3-8\lambda\alpha^2-16\lambda h^2
       -8h\alpha^2\Omega .
\end{aligned}
 \label{eq:coeff}
\end{equation}
These three coefficients are nonnegative. For $e_3$, replacing its
last term by $-16|h|\alpha^2$ gives an increasing cubic on
$\lambda\ge\beta$, whose value at $\beta$ is
$16|\alpha|(|\alpha|+2|h|)^2$.
If $D\ge0$ too, the polynomial is positive for every $r>0$. A negative
eigenvalue $-r$ of $\lambda I-H$ would make it zero, a contradiction.

It remains to compare the determinants for different measurements. Set $x=(1-c)/2$, $y=(1-d)/2$, and write
$D(x,y)=\det(\lambda I-H)$. Direct expansion gives
\begin{equation}
 D(x,y)=F(xy)+64h^2(4h^2-\alpha^2)xy(x+y),
 \label{eq:det}
\end{equation}
where $F$ is a polynomial in $xy$. For $|\alpha|\le|h|$,
the coefficient of the sum is nonnegative. At fixed product,
$x+y\ge2\sqrt{xy}$, so
\begin{equation}
 D(x,y)\ge D(\sqrt{xy},\sqrt{xy}).
 \label{eq:agm}
\end{equation}
The equal-overlap point remains in $[0,1]^2$. If the bound holds at every equal-overlap pair, this comparison proves
it at every pair. Equal overlaps therefore attain the maximum.

For $|\alpha|\ge|h|$, the maximum is the local bound $\beta$.
The endpoint tilted-CHSH inequality follows from the identity
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
4I-(C_{\mathrm{CHSH}}+2A_0)
={}&(I-C-A_1D_B)^2\\
&+\tfrac14\sum_{y=0}^1[(I-A_0)(I+B_y)]^2,
\end{split}
 \label{eq:critical}
\end{equation}
where $C=(B_0+B_1)/2$ and $D_B=(B_0-B_1)/2$.
Party exchange and output flips give either sign of CHSH and
either sign of the marginal. Average the Alice and Bob bounds,
multiply by $|h|$, and use $\pm(A_0+B_0)\le2I$ for the remaining weight
$|\alpha|-|h|$. This proves $H\le\beta I$, attained by deterministic
measurements with equal overlaps. The reduction thus holds for every
real $h,\alpha$.

\emph{One angle gives one finite lift.}---
At $c=d$, $H$ preserves the singlet
$|\psi_-\rangle=(|01\rangle-|10\rangle)/\sqrt2$, with eigenvalue
$-2hc$. Use the ordered basis $(|\phi_+\rangle,|\phi_-\rangle,|\psi_+\rangle)$
for the triplet subspace, where
$|\phi_\pm\rangle=(|00\rangle\pm|11\rangle)/\sqrt2$ and
$|\psi_+\rangle=(|01\rangle+|10\rangle)/\sqrt2$. Set
$c=(1-t^2)/(1+t^2)$, initially with $t\ge0$. Multiplying this block
of $\lambda I-H$ by $(1+t^2)^2$ removes the denominators and gives
\begin{equation}
 K(t)=
 \begin{pmatrix}
 \lambda p_0-2hp_1&-2\alpha p_0&0\\
 -2\alpha p_0&\lambda p_0-2hp_2&-8ht^3\\
 0&-8ht^3&\lambda p_0+2hp_2
 \end{pmatrix},
 \label{eq:K}
\end{equation}
with $p_0=(1+t^2)^2$, $p_1=1-t^4$ and $p_2=1+4t^2-t^4$.
At $t=0$ and in the leading coefficient, positivity forces
$\lambda\ge2h+2|\alpha|$ and $\lambda\ge-2h+2|\alpha|$.
Together they imply $\lambda\ge\beta$, so the singlet bound holds too.
Continuity includes $c=-1$, while
$K(-t)=RK(t)R$, $R=\operatorname{diag}(1,1,-1)$.
Consequently, for all real support coefficients,
\begin{equation}
 hS+\alpha M\le\lambda\ \text{on }\W
 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
 K(t)\succeq0\quad\forall t\in\mathbb R .
 \label{eq:equiv}
\end{equation}

Positivity for all $t$ has a finite description. A real matrix polynomial
in one variable is PSD everywhere exactly when
$K(t)=\sum_j Q_j(t)^TQ_j(t)$ for polynomial matrices $Q_j$, including
singular cases~\cite{HanselkaSinn}. Each diagonal entry of $K$ is a sum
of real squares of degree at most four, so every entry of $Q_j$ has degree
at most two: higher leading powers cannot cancel.

For a real vector $\xi=(\xi_1,\xi_2,\xi_3)^T$, the scalar
$\xi^TK(t)\xi$ is therefore a sum of squares of linear combinations
of the nine monomials $t^k\xi_i$, $0\le k\le2$. Equivalently, it is
a quadratic form in those monomials with a PSD coefficient matrix,
called a Gram matrix. Average this representation under
$(t,\xi)\mapsto(-t,R\xi)$. Terms that change sign under this transformation cancel,
leaving two groups of monomials:
\[
\begin{aligned}
 Z_+&=(\xi_1,\xi_2,t\xi_3,t^2\xi_1,t^2\xi_2)^T,\\
 Z_-&=(\xi_3,t\xi_1,t\xi_2,t^2\xi_3)^T.
\end{aligned}
\]
Equation~\eqref{eq:equiv} is therefore equivalent to the polynomial identity
\begin{equation}
 \xi^TK(t)\xi=Z_+^TGZ_++Z_-^TJZ_-,
 \qquad G,J\succeq0 .
 \label{eq:gram}
\end{equation}
We now use the same monomials to test whether a pair $(M,S)$ belongs
to $\W$. Replace evaluation of polynomials by a linear functional. The entries of
$Z_+Z_+^T$ and $Z_-Z_-^T$ span a sixteen-dimensional polynomial
space. Let $\mathcal L_z$ assign real values $z\in\mathbb R^{16}$
to a basis of that space, extend it linearly, and apply it entrywise to set
\[
 U(z)=\mathcal L_z(Z_+Z_+^T),\qquad
 V(z)=\mathcal L_z(Z_-Z_-^T).
\]
Define the three linear coordinate maps by
\[
 \mathcal L_z(\xi^TK(t)\xi)
 =\lambda\ell(z)-\alpha m(z)-hs(z).
\]
If $U(z),V(z)\succeq0$ and $\ell(z)=1$, applying
$\mathcal L_z$ to Eq.~\eqref{eq:gram} gives
\begin{equation}
 \lambda-\alpha m(z)-hs(z)
 =\operatorname{tr}(UG)+\operatorname{tr}(VJ)\ge0.
 \label{eq:pairing}
\end{equation}
Thus $(m(z),s(z))$ satisfies every supporting inequality of $\W$.

Conversely, each real unit triplet state $\xi$ at angle $t$ supplies
the functional $P\mapsto P(t,\xi)/(1+t^2)^2$. It gives
\begin{equation}
 U=\frac{Z_+Z_+^T}{(1+t^2)^2},\qquad
 V=\frac{Z_-Z_-^T}{(1+t^2)^2},
 \label{eq:witness}
\end{equation}
with $\ell(z)=1$ and coordinates equal to the state's $M,S$. The scaled features have limits at $t=\infty$.
The pairs $(M,S)$ obtained this way form a compact set. Their convex
hull has the same maxima of $hS+\alpha M$ as $\W$: equal overlaps
suffice, and each
singlet point $(0,-2c)$ is a mixture of the triplet endpoint
points $(0,\pm2)$. Their convex hull is therefore $\W$.
Mixing the corresponding functionals gives
\begin{equation}
 \W=\{(m(z),s(z)):\ell(z)=1,\ U(z),V(z)\succeq0\}.
 \label{eq:lift}
\end{equation}
The complete proofs give every entry of $U,V$~\cite{SM}. The set of
triplet pairs is also connected. Every point in the convex hull of a
compact connected planar set lies on a segment joining two points of
the set~\cite{SM}. Thus every $(M,S)$ is a mixture of two real
equal-overlap strategies. Each matrix is then a sum of two rank-one
matrices, so $U,V$ can simultaneously have rank at most two.

\emph{Infinite depth, minimal block order.}---
Equal overlaps attain the quantum maximum. Finite NPA certificates,
however, must hold for independent measurement angles. Let
$f(\alpha)=\max_{\W}(S+\alpha M)$ and let $f_L(\alpha)$ be its
level-$L$ NPA upper bound. The NPA error is $f_L-f$; the quantum
violation is $f-(2+2|\alpha|)$. The preceding proof~\cite{prior}
shows that a bounded ratio of these quantities as $\alpha\uparrow1$
would give a sum of polynomial squares for
\[
 \mathfrak m(X,Y)+c,\qquad
 \mathfrak m=1+X^4Y^2+X^2Y^4-3X^2Y^2,\quad c\ge0.
\]
Here $X,Y$ are rescaled real angle variables. Such a sum of squares is impossible:
constant values on both axes restrict each
cubic square factor to $a+bXY+dX^2Y+eXY^2$, whose squared
$X^2Y^2$ coefficient is nonnegative. Yet on equal angles,
\[
 \mathfrak m(X,X)=(X^2-1)^2(2X^2+1).
\]
The two-angle polynomial is not a sum of squares, although its
equal-angle restriction is. The complete rescaling and limit argument
appears in the companion~\cite{SM}.

The quantum maximum satisfies~\cite{GigenaEtAl2025,prior}
\[
 f(1-T)=4-2T+\tfrac43T^3+O(T^4),\qquad T\downarrow0.
\]
For a polygon, this maximum would be affine in $T$ sufficiently near
zero. Thus $\W$ is not a polygon. PSD constraints are polynomial
inequalities; their projections are semialgebraic, meaning finite
unions of sets defined by polynomial equalities and inequalities.
Scheiderer's theorem gives extension degree at most two for every
closed convex semialgebraic set in the plane~\cite{Scheiderer}.
Scalar PSD constraints describe polyhedra, so size one cannot suffice.
Hence $\sxdeg(\W)=2$.

The construction also gives thirty-two exact projections.
For each CHSH sign pattern $\chi_{ij}\in\{\pm1\}$ with
$\prod_{ij}\chi_{ij}=-1$,
and each selected input pair $x,y$, relabelling inputs and outputs identifies
\[
 \left(a_x+\chi_{xy}b_y,\ \sum_{ij}\chi_{ij}E_{ij}\right)
\]
with the same region $\W$.
Adding these lifts to any NPA level retains every quantum correlation
and gives the exact maximum of every linear combination of the two
coordinates on each plane. This removes the NPA error on all
thirty-two planes.
