\twocolumn[{\centering
{\Large\bfseries Infinite NPA depth, finite semidefinite lift\par}
\vspace{5pt}
{\normalsize An exact quantum region in the simplest Bell experiment\par}
\vspace{13pt}}]

The quantum region of the CHSH value and the sum of two marginal averages requires infinite Navascués–Pironio–Acín depth, yet admits an exact finite semidefinite lift. We construct this lift by showing that every supporting bound is attained with equal measurement overlaps: one angle determines the entire boundary. Finite NPA certificates must remain valid for two independently varying angles, where the obstruction to finite depth survives. The same region has the smallest semidefinite extension degree possible for a nonpolyhedral set, separating infinite hierarchy depth from minimal semidefinite complexity.

Alice and Bob share a quantum state and choose between observables
$A_x,B_y$ with outcomes $\pm1$, where $x,y\in\{0,1\}$. Write $a_x=\langle A_x\rangle$, $b_y=\langle B_y\rangle$ and
$E_{xy}=\langle A_xB_y\rangle$. Retain
\begin{equation}
 M=a_0+b_0,\qquad S=E_{00}+E_{01}+E_{10}-E_{11}.
 \label{eq:coordinates}
\end{equation}
Let $\W$ contain all pairs $(M,S)$ attainable with quantum states
and local measurements. The marginal averages need not be equal.
For its level-$L$ NPA outer approximation $\W_L$, the preceding
Letter~\cite{prior} and the construction below give
\begin{equation}
 \boxed{\begin{gathered}
 \W\subsetneq\W_L\quad\text{for every finite }L,\\
 \sxdeg(\W)=2.
 \end{gathered}}
 \label{eq:result}
\end{equation}
A semidefinite lift describes membership by positive semidefinite (PSD)
matrices whose entries are affine in $(M,S)$ and auxiliary variables.
We construct one with matrix sizes five and four. The extension degree
is the smallest possible largest matrix size; its value two follows
from a theorem on planar convex sets.

\emph{Why one angle suffices.}
To determine $\W$, consider an arbitrary supporting inequality
$hS+\alpha M\leq\lambda$, with real $h,\alpha$.
After realizing the measurements projectively on a larger space,
Jordan's lemma reduces this question to whether the two-qubit matrices
$\lambda I-H$ are PSD. Here
\[
\begin{gathered}
 H=h\,\mathrm{CHSH}+\alpha(ZI+IZ),\\
 A_1=cZ+\sqrt{1-c^2}X,\quad B_1=dZ+\sqrt{1-d^2}X.
\end{gathered}
\]
Here $X,Z$ are Pauli matrices, $A_0=B_0=Z$, and
$\mathrm{CHSH}=A_0B_0+A_0B_1+A_1B_0-A_1B_1$. Products of local
operators mean tensor products. The numbers $c,d\in[-1,1]$ are
the cosines of the angles between each party's two measurement directions. Deterministic strategies attain
$\beta=2|h|+2|\alpha|$, so $\lambda\geq\beta$ is necessary.
Above this bound, the first three elementary symmetric functions of
the eigenvalues of $\lambda I-H$ are nonnegative. Its determinant
therefore decides positivity: if it too is nonnegative, the characteristic
polynomial of $H-\lambda I$ has nonnegative coefficients and hence
no positive root.

Put $x=(1-c)/2$, $y=(1-d)/2$, and write
$D(x,y)=\det(\lambda I-H)$. The determinant takes the form
\begin{equation}
 D(x,y)=F(xy)+64h^2(4h^2-\alpha^2)xy(x+y),
 \label{eq:key}
\end{equation}
where $F$ depends on the product alone.
In the nonclassical range $|\alpha|\leq|h|$, the coefficient of $x+y$
is nonnegative. At fixed product, $x+y\geq2\sqrt{xy}$, with equality
at $x=y=\sqrt{xy}$. Thus equal overlaps minimize the determinant;
positivity there implies positivity for every pair.
For $|\alpha|\geq|h|$, the maximum is the local bound $\beta$:
average the Alice and Bob tilted-CHSH bounds at their local threshold,
then use $\pm(A_0+B_0)\le2I$ for the remaining marginal coefficient.
Equal overlaps attain this value too, for every real $h,\alpha$.

At $c=d$, use the Bell states $\phi_\pm=(|00\rangle\pm|11\rangle)/\sqrt2$
and $\psi_\pm=(|01\rangle\pm|10\rangle)/\sqrt2$. The singlet $\psi_-$
decouples. In the ordered triplet basis $(\phi_+,\phi_-,\psi_+)$, set
$c=(1-t^2)/(1+t^2)$ and multiply $\lambda I-H$ by $(1+t^2)^2$. With
\[
 p_0=(1+t^2)^2,\qquad p_1=1-t^4,\qquad p_2=1+4t^2-t^4,
\]
the result is the quartic matrix
\begin{equation}
 K(t)=
 \begin{pmatrix}
 \lambda p_0-2hp_1&-2\alpha p_0&0\\
 -2\alpha p_0&\lambda p_0-2hp_2&-8ht^3\\
 0&-8ht^3&\lambda p_0+2hp_2
 \end{pmatrix}.
 \label{eq:K}
\end{equation}
Its value at zero and leading coefficient force $\lambda\geq\beta$,
so the singlet bound holds too. Continuity includes $c=-1$, while
$K(-t)=RK(t)R$, $R=\operatorname{diag}(1,1,-1)$, includes negative $t$. We have therefore proved
\begin{equation}
 \max_{\W}(hS+\alpha M)\leq\lambda
 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad K(t)\succeq0\quad\forall t\in\mathbb R .
 \label{eq:support}
\end{equation}
The entries are affine---constant plus linear---in $\lambda,\alpha,h$.
Thus one fixed matrix describes every supporting inequality.

\emph{From one variable to one finite lift.}
A real matrix polynomial in one variable is PSD everywhere exactly
when it equals $\sum_j Q_j(t)^TQ_j(t)$ for polynomial matrices $Q_j$,
including singular cases~\cite{hs}. Quartic diagonal entries force
each $Q_j$ to have degree at most two. Thus, for $\xi\in\mathbb R^3$,
$\xi^TK(t)\xi$ is a sum of squares of linear combinations of
$t^k\xi_i$, $0\le k\le2$. Averaging under $(t,\xi)\mapsto(-t,R\xi)$
cancels terms that change sign and leaves two groups of monomials:
\[
\begin{aligned}
 Z_+&=(\xi_1,\xi_2,t\xi_3,t^2\xi_1,t^2\xi_2)^T,\\
 Z_-&=(\xi_3,t\xi_1,t\xi_2,t^2\xi_3)^T .
\end{aligned}
\]
Consequently Eq.~\eqref{eq:support} is equivalent to the polynomial identity
\begin{equation}
 \begin{gathered}
 \xi^TK(t)\xi=Z_+^TGZ_++Z_-^TJZ_-,\\
 G\succeq0,\quad J\succeq0,
 \end{gathered}
 \label{eq:gram}
\end{equation}
The PSD coefficient matrices $G,J$, of sizes five and four, are
called Gram matrices. Matching coefficients gives sixteen affine equations.

Let $\mathcal L_z$ be a linear functional on the sixteen-dimensional
span of the entries of $Z_+Z_+^T,Z_-Z_-^T$, specified by sixteen real
values $z$. Apply it entrywise to define
\[
 U(z)=\mathcal L_z(Z_+Z_+^T),\quad V(z)=\mathcal L_z(Z_-Z_-^T),
\]
and define $\ell,m,s$ by
$\mathcal L_z(\xi^TK\xi)=\lambda\ell(z)-\alpha m(z)-hs(z)$.
Then the membership test is
\begin{equation}
 \W=\{(m(z),s(z)):\ell(z)=1,\ U(z),V(z)\succeq0\}.
 \label{eq:membership}
\end{equation}
The complete proofs list every matrix entry.

There is a direct physical proof of the reverse inclusion.
For a real unit triplet state $\xi$ at angle $t$, choose
\[
 U=\frac{Z_+Z_+^T}{(1+t^2)^2},\qquad
 V=\frac{Z_-Z_-^T}{(1+t^2)^2}.
\]
These matrices come from $\mathcal L_z(P)=P(t,\xi)/(1+t^2)^2$,
with $\ell(z)=1$ and coordinates equal to the state's $M,S$. Their limits include $t=\infty$.
The triplet states attain every quantum maximum of $hS+\alpha M$; the omitted
singlet points $(0,-2c)$ are mixtures of triplet endpoints $(0,\pm2)$.
Their convex hull is therefore $\W$. Mixing witnesses admits every
point. Conversely, pairing any feasible matrices with every Gram
certificate gives $\lambda-hS-\alpha M\ge0$, so no point outside
$\W$ is admitted.

The set of pairs obtained from triplet states is compact and connected. A point in its planar convex
hull lies on a chord joining two image points: otherwise its connected
radial image would lie in an open semicircle, giving a separating line.
Thus every point of $\W$ needs at most two real equal-overlap strategies,
with both membership matrices of rank at most two.

\emph{Why the NPA obstruction remains.}
Equal overlaps determine support values; a finite NPA certificate
must represent the bound for independently chosen measurement angles.
Write $f_L(\alpha)$ for the NPA maximum and $f(\alpha)$ for the quantum
maximum of $S+\alpha M$. As $\alpha\uparrow1$, a bounded ratio
$[f_L-f]/[f-(2+2|\alpha|)]$ would force a shifted Motzkin polynomial
to be a sum of squares~\cite{prior}. The numerator is the NPA error;
the denominator is the quantum violation above the local bound.
The complete earlier argument is retained in the companion.

\emph{Minimal block order and thirty-two exact projections.}
For $f(\alpha)=\max_\W(S+\alpha M)$, the exact quantum bound has
the expansion~\cite{prior}
\begin{equation}
 f(1-T)=4-2T+\tfrac43T^3+O(T^4),\qquad T\downarrow0 .
 \label{eq:cubic}
\end{equation}
For a polygon, this maximum would be affine sufficiently close to $\alpha=1$,
contrary to Eq.~\eqref{eq:cubic}. Thus $\W$ is nonpolyhedral.
Its finite lift makes it semialgebraic: it is a finite union of sets
defined by polynomial equalities and inequalities. Since it is also
closed and convex, Scheiderer's planar theorem gives $\sxdeg(\W)\leq2$~\cite{soc}.
Order-one lifts are polyhedral, hence $\sxdeg(\W)=2$.
The displayed lift has sizes $5,4$; the theorem guarantees a lift
using matrices of size at most two.

Physical input and output relabellings give the same quantum region for
\[
 \left(a_x+\chi_{xy}b_y,\ \sum_{ij}\chi_{ij}E_{ij}\right),
 \qquad \prod_{ij}\chi_{ij}=-1.
\]
Here $\chi_{ij}\in\{\pm1\}$. The eight CHSH sign patterns and four selected input pairs give
32 distinct planes. Intersecting their fixed lifts with any NPA
relaxation makes every support value on these planes exact, including
all the directions responsible for finite-level nontermination.
